# Conclusion: tactical and strategic programming with AI

This is a synthesis of the [source notes](sources/) for the human to read and discuss. It records what the sources say, how well they support it and what is unverified. It is not a position. Every note was re-read from the raw page, PDF or API response and corrected; each note says how it was read. Most are full reads. Exceptions: the GitClear full report is form-gated and unread, the Ousterhout and Kent Beck episodes and the Pragmatic Engineer Pocock episode were seen only as the newsletter's written summary (no transcript loaded), the DORA and agentic-refactoring reports were read in part, and no video was watched.

## Where the pair of terms comes from

The terms are John Ousterhout's, and the pair is credited to his book *A Philosophy of Software Design* by Pocock and others. The one primary text read is his Stanford CS190 (Winter 2018) lecture page, a short outline ([CS190 lecture](sources/ousterhout-cs190-working-isnt-good-enough.md)). There, tactical programming's goal is "to get the next feature or bug fix working", accepting shortcuts and kludges, and the page states the result as bad design and high complexity. Strategic programming's primary goal is "a great design", achieved through "continual small investments". The investment level is "as much as you can afford" with "10-20% overhead?", and payback is "6-12 months?". Both figures carry a question mark on the page, so they are the author's tentative guesses, not measurements. "Tactical tornadoes" appears on that page only as an unelaborated bullet. The principle is "Working isn't good enough."

The book's own text was never read. The only book-based source is a reader's highlights ([book ch. 3 notes](sources/ousterhout-book-ch3-tactical-tornado.md)), which give the definitions of tactical and strategic, the tactical tornado (a prolific developer whose fast, purely tactical output is "rarely considered heroes by engineers who must work with their code in the future") and "working code isn't enough". They do not contain the 10-20% figure, so that figure is sourced to the lecture page only and is unverified for the book.

The important feature of the original, on both sources, is that it describes a stance of one programmer toward the code they write, over time. It is not a division by level of abstraction and not a division of labour between people.

## How Pocock adapts it

Pocock's adaptation is a division of labour. Support differs by where he said it.

- X thread, 2026-05-20, read in full through the fxtwitter API ([X thread](sources/pocock-x-tactical-vs-strategic-thread.md)). It never names Ousterhout. Tactical is on the ground and short-term (syntax, file structure, how to test changes); strategic is long-term (architecture, automating away problems). "Agents have eaten the tactical part of programming", "AI cannot code strategically", and a developer's day is "100% strategy". He worries about juniors, whose old route into strategic experience ran through tactical tasks, and ends with open questions on how to train strategic thinking. The imagery (soldier, general, war) is his.
- Interview by David Ondrej, third-party auto-transcript, accuracy against the video unchecked ([transcript](sources/pocock-agentic-workflow-transcript-ousterhout-credit.md)). At about 01:50 he credits Ousterhout's book for the tactical/strategic difference. Same framing: AI has "eaten" tactical programming because it does it cheaper, and you need to be great at strategic programming to get the most from "this infinite fleet of tactical programmers". At about 04:00 he says strategic programming "really hasn't changed", since the delegation is now to AI instead of to junior or mid-level programmers.
- AI Engineer Europe 2026 talk, read from the page's timestamped transcript ([talk](sources/pocock-ai-engineer-software-fundamentals-talk.md)). At 17:16 he calls AI "a kind of tactical programmer, a sergeant on the ground" and says you need someone "thinking on the strategic level". He does not name Ousterhout in that passage and does not present it as the Ousterhout pair. Elsewhere in the talk he credits Ousterhout for the definition of complexity and for deep modules.
- Pragmatic Engineer episode, 17 Sep 2026 ([episode](sources/pocock-pragmatic-engineer-ai-skills-episode.md)). The page's transcript did not load. The line pairing Ousterhout's categorisation with agents being "more than capable of tactical programming" is the newsletter author's takeaway, using the word "borrowing". It is a second-hand summary, not a Pocock quote.
- His skills repo README never uses "tactical" or "strategic"; it cites Ousterhout only for deep modules ([skills repo](sources/pocock-skills-repo-ousterhout-mention.md)).

Qualifications. The mapping is his own, not Ousterhout's. His cut also differs: his thread files file structure and how to test changes under tactical, while for Ousterhout the design of modules is strategic, and the talk itself leans on Ousterhout's deep modules, a design concern. And where he credits Ousterhout for the pair (the interview) he does not say he is changing its meaning.

Ousterhout on AI: the Pragmatic Engineer newsletter's takeaways (host's words) say AI coding tools and agents are "akin to 'tactical tornadoes'" that code fast while adding issues and tech debt, and that Ousterhout does not see current tools replacing high-level design. The summary also says AI shifts engineers toward architecture and decomposition and may make design more important. This is the host's framing; only the written summary was read, with no transcript, so it is not confirmed that Ousterhout used "tactical tornado" about AI ([Ousterhout episode](sources/ousterhout-pragmatic-engineer-episode.md)). The episode does have a chapter titled "Tactical tornadoes vs. 10x engineers" (07:20).

## Do the sources support "AI is good at tactical, humans do strategic"?

Split into two claims: what people do in practice, and what AI can reliably do.

**Observed division of labour: reasonably supported, with caveats.**
- Anthropic's study of its own engineers (132 surveyed, 53 interviews, 200,000 transcripts; self-selected, vendor) says engineers delegate tasks that are easily verifiable, low-stakes or boring, and consistently did not use Claude for high-level or strategic thinking or design decisions needing organisational context or "taste". Design and planning showed the least productivity gain. More than half said they can fully delegate only 0-20% of their work. The authors call the boundary a "moving target" and report relatively more design and planning use than six months earlier ([Anthropic work study](sources/anthropic-how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic.md)). The basis for delegation is verifiability and tedium, not shown AI quality.
- The agentic-refactoring preprint (15,451 refactorings in 12,256 PRs, open-source Java, AIDev dataset) reports agents lean toward low-level, localised edits more than humans do: low-level 35.8% versus 24.4%, high-level 43.0% versus 54.9%, medium about equal. So agents still do many high-level refactorings. The human baseline comes from earlier work, not the same dataset. Agent refactorings brought small structural improvements but did not consistently reduce smells (negligible effect size). It describes what agents did in sampled PRs, not what they can do, and its "delegate routine cleanup, keep design-level work" line is the authors' recommendation ([agentic refactoring](sources/arxiv-agentic-refactoring-empirical-study.md)).
- Anthropic's Claude Code guidance has the human explore, plan, specify, verify and course-correct while Claude implements, and treats code-level output as untrustworthy until verified. It says to skip planning when the diff fits in one sentence. Vendor guidance, no tactical/strategic wording ([best practices](sources/anthropic-claude-code-best-practices.md)).
- Kent Beck's first-hand account (single person, single project) has him keeping design and complexity control and stopping the genie coding ahead, while also supervising at test and code level. He lists loops, unrequested functionality and test deletion or disabling as warning signs, and he is unhappy with the resulting code quality ([augmented coding](sources/beck-augmented-coding-beyond-the-vibes.md)). The Pragmatic Engineer summary of his interview, in the host's words, also reports trouble stopping agents deleting tests ([interview summary](sources/beck-pragmatic-engineer-tdd-ai-agents.md)). In 2026 he describes managing multiple agents as holding state in his head, a coordination burden and an unsolved problem, in a post sponsored by the tool vendor; it says nothing on tactical or strategic ([nobody wants agents](sources/beck-genie-lessons-nobody-wants-agents.md)).
- Böckeler (martinfowler.com, April 2026) frames the human job as steering the agent by iterating on the harness and directing human input where it matters most. Agents lack accountability, taste and organisational memory, and confidence in functional behaviour, and in catching overengineering and misdiagnosis, is not yet high enough to reduce supervision ([harness engineering](sources/boeckeler-harness-engineering-human-role.md)).

**AI capability at tactical work: mixed and partly contradicted.**
- METR's 2025 randomized trial (16 experienced developers, 246 issues, Cursor with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet) found tasks took 19% longer with AI, while developers still believed AI had sped them up by 20%. METR reports similar PR quality. Results are specific to experienced developers in familiar, high-standard repositories, and the page now carries an "out of date" banner ([METR 2025](sources/metr-early-2025-experienced-developer-rct.md)).
- METR's 2026 follow-up (57 developers, 800+ tasks) reports speedups: -18% (CI -38% to +9%) for returning developers and -4% (CI -15% to +9%) for new ones, where a negative number means less time; both intervals include zero. METR calls this an unreliable signal: many developers refused to work without AI and 30-50% withheld tasks, which it says likely biases the estimate downward. It says developers are likely more sped up than in early 2025 but its data is very weak evidence for the size, and it is redesigning the study ([METR 2026](sources/metr-2026-uplift-design-update.md)). So there is no reliable 2026 measurement, and its direction is toward speedup.
- DORA 2025 (survey of nearly 5,000 professionals, self-reported, association not causation) finds AI adoption associated with higher delivery throughput and product performance and still with higher delivery instability, and with higher code quality, where 59% perceive improved code quality. Its blog says without strong testing, version control and fast feedback, more change volume leads to instability. The headline is that AI amplifies an organisation's existing strengths and weaknesses ([DORA 2025](sources/dora-2025-ai-assisted-software-development.md)).

**AI weakness at design-level qualities: supported but thinly, and contested.**
- GitClear (vendor, observational; only the abstract-level claims were readable) reports, over 211M changed lines from 2020-2024, refactoring-associated lines falling from 25% of changed lines in 2021 to under 10% in 2024 and cloned lines rising from 8.3% to 12.3%. The page does not state whether or how AI-authored lines were identified, so the trend is not shown to be caused by AI ([GitClear](sources/gitclear-2025-ai-code-quality.md)).
- A May 2026 preprint (Python; Experiment I: 90 problems, five LLMs; Experiment II: about 20 repositories from one framework and one model) reports that lines of code correlate strongly with architectural smells (rho 0.94), that prompt detail had no significant effect, and that running code was as likely to be structurally flawed as failed code. Its claim that decay grows with model capability rests on a few models and small counts. Static-analysis smells are the quality proxy, and Experiment II has no human baseline ([AI-generated smells](sources/arxiv-ai-generated-smells-code-and-architecture.md)).
- A registered report with a similar-sounding title is a study plan with no results and must not be cited as evidence of an effect ([registered report](sources/arxiv-ai-writes-code-humans-pay-the-debt.md)).
- Against these, DORA's perception data points the other way on code quality, and METR 2025 found similar PR quality with and without AI.

Taken together: the sources agree on a rough behavioural pattern (humans keep design, planning and steering; agents write code) and mostly agree that strategic attention matters more when generation is cheap. They do not establish that AI is reliably good at tactical work. Several point the other way: test tampering, unrequested scope, code-level taste gaps, supervision still required, and a 2025 measured slowdown, while the 2026 evidence leans toward speedup but is weak. The Anthropic study also names a "paradox of supervision", where supervising requires coding skills that may atrophy. The junior-pipeline worry in Pocock's thread is an opinion with no evidence attached in the sources.

## Connection to AI coding

- The original Ousterhout distinction reappears in AI form as a risk in the newsletter host's framing of the Ousterhout episode: an agent optimised to make things work, fast, resembles a tactical tornado. The duplication, refactoring decline and smell evidence is consistent with that, but the attribution to Ousterhout is unconfirmed and no source tests the reading directly.
- The strategic side that the sources name is a mix: architecture and design, planning and specification, harness and process design, coordination of agents, verification, and (DORA) organisational strategy and platform quality. None of these is the same as Ousterhout's strategic programming, which concerns the design quality of code one writes.
- Several sources tie architecture to how well AI works. Böckeler says committing to a topology makes a harness more achievable. DORA's blog and foreword give an Adidas case study, a company anecdote and not a survey finding, where teams in loosely coupled architectures with fast feedback saw 20-30% gains and teams tightly coupled to ERP systems saw little or none. Pocock argues deep modules help both humans and AI, as an assertion in a talk.

## Gaps and unverified points

- The book's text was not read; the 10-20% and payback figures are from the lecture page with question marks, and "tactical tornado" is confirmed only as a bullet there and in a reader's notes.
- Whether Ousterhout himself said AI tools are "tactical tornadoes" is unverified (no transcript of the episode); likewise the Pragmatic Engineer Pocock episode's Ousterhout pairing is the newsletter author's takeaway, not a Pocock quote, as its transcript did not load.
- The Ondrej transcript is third-party auto-transcription and unchecked against the video; the videos and the talk video were not watched, and X thread replies were not read.
- GitClear's full report was unreadable, and the DORA report and the agentic-refactoring paper were read in part.
- Both preprints are not peer-reviewed as far as the PDFs show.
- No source tests the split itself, for example by comparing outcomes when humans do strategic work and delegate tactical work versus other arrangements.
- Whether the split holds for less experienced developers, non-web, non-Java or non-Python code, or later models is not covered.
