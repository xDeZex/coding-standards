# Conclusion: tactical and strategic programming with AI

This is a synthesis of the [source notes](sources/) for the human to read and discuss. It records what the sources say, how well they support it and what is unverified. It is not a position. Almost every source was read through a summariser or as a summary page, not in full, and the notes say so; quotes should be checked against the originals before they are reused.

## Where the pair of terms comes from

The terms are John Ousterhout's. In *A Philosophy of Software Design* and the Stanford CS190 course it grew out of, tactical programming aims to get the next feature or bug fix working, accepting shortcuts that add complexity. Strategic programming's primary goal is a great design that also works, paid for by continual small investments. The benchmark is 10-20% of time. Ousterhout guesses these investments pay back in 6-12 months, and the question mark is in his own slide, so this is an estimate and not a measurement. His extreme case is the "tactical tornado", a prolific developer whose fast output leaves a mess for others. The foundational line is "working isn't good enough". Support: the lecture page is a primary source ([CS190 lecture](sources/ousterhout-cs190-working-isnt-good-enough.md)). The book chapter was only seen through a reader's highlights ([book ch. 3](sources/ousterhout-book-ch3-tactical-tornado.md)), and the 10-20% figure attributed to the chapter is unchecked there.

The important feature of the original is that it describes a stance of one programmer toward the code they write, over time. It is not a division by level of abstraction and not a division of labour between people. Both Ousterhout notes say this explicitly.

## How Pocock adapts it

Matt Pocock names Ousterhout's book as the source and, per a third-party summary, describes himself as "borrowing" the categorisation ([Pragmatic Engineer episode](sources/pocock-pragmatic-engineer-ai-skills-episode.md); [video transcript](sources/pocock-agentic-workflow-transcript-ousterhout-credit.md); [conference talk](sources/pocock-ai-engineer-software-fundamentals-talk.md)). His adaptation is a division of labour. Tactical means the on-the-ground work: writing code, syntax, debugging, commits. Strategic means architecture, longer-term thinking and, in the X thread, automation. He claims AI has "eaten" tactical programming because it is cheaper, that AI cannot program strategically, and that a developer's day is now "100% strategy". The thread also worries about juniors, whose route into strategic skill ran through tactical tasks ([X thread](sources/pocock-x-tactical-vs-strategic-thread.md)).

Three qualifications. First, the mapping is his own, not Ousterhout's. Second, his cut differs from Ousterhout's: he files file structure and how to test changes under tactical, while for Ousterhout module design is strategic. The talk also leans on Ousterhout's deep modules, which are a design concern, so his own material is not fully consistent with the split. In his skills repo he cites Ousterhout only for deep modules and never uses the tactical/strategic pair ([skills repo](sources/pocock-skills-repo-ousterhout-mention.md)). Third, support quality: the X thread page returned HTTP 402 and was seen only as a search snippet; the talk was read as a conference summary; the episode as a newsletter summary; the transcript is a third-party site of unchecked accuracy. What Pocock argues is well attested across these; the exact wording is not.

Ousterhout himself, as summarised by the Pragmatic Engineer newsletter, uses his term differently for AI: as a risk. AI code generation may resemble tactical tornadoes, fast output that adds complexity, and he reportedly thinks AI shifts engineers toward architecture and decomposition and makes design more important ([Ousterhout episode](sources/ousterhout-pragmatic-engineer-episode.md)). Only the newsletter's summary was read; whether Ousterhout said "tactical tornado" about AI is unverified.

## Do the sources support "AI is good at tactical, humans do strategic"?

Split into two claims: what people do in practice, and what AI can reliably do.

**Observed division of labour: reasonably supported, with caveats.**
- Anthropic's study of its own engineers reports that they keep high-level design and delegate work that is verifiable, low-stakes, boring or outside their expertise. Design and planning showed the least productivity gain, and most said they can fully delegate only 0-20% of work. It is a vendor self-report read through a summariser ([Anthropic work study](sources/anthropic-how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic.md)). Note the basis for delegation is verifiability and tedium, not a finding that AI is good at it.
- An empirical study of 15,451 agent refactorings in open-source Java PRs finds agents do mostly low-level, localised edits (renames, variable types), where humans do high-level design changes ([agentic refactoring](sources/arxiv-agentic-refactoring-empirical-study.md)). This describes what agents did in sampled PRs, not what they are capable of. Abstract only, preprint.
- Anthropic's Claude Code guidance has the human explore, plan, specify, verify and course-correct while Claude implements, but treats code-level output as untrustworthy until verified and never claims tactical competence ([best practices](sources/anthropic-claude-code-best-practices.md)). It also says to skip planning when the diff fits in one sentence, so the strategic layer is scaled to the task. Vendor guidance, not independent evidence.
- Kent Beck (first-hand accounts) keeps design and complexity control, and stops the AI coding ahead. But he also supervises at test and code level, and lists unrequested features and test deletion or disabling as failure modes ([augmented coding](sources/beck-augmented-coding-beyond-the-vibes.md); [Pragmatic Engineer interview, summary only](sources/beck-pragmatic-engineer-tdd-ai-agents.md)). In a later post he describes the human role as coordination and holding state across multiple agents, and presents this as a burden and unsolved problem, not a settled division ([nobody wants agents](sources/beck-genie-lessons-nobody-wants-agents.md)).
- Böckeler frames the human job as steering the agent by improving the harness and directing human input to where it matters most, with architecture affecting how controllable the agent is. She also says agents lack taste and accountability at code level and that confidence in functional behaviour is not yet high enough to reduce supervision ([harness engineering](sources/boeckeler-harness-engineering-human-role.md)).

**AI capability at tactical work: mixed and partly contradicted.**
- METR's 2025 randomized trial (16 experienced developers, 246 issues, early-2025 tools) found a 19% slowdown with AI, while developers believed they were sped up by about 20%. The results are specific to experienced developers on familiar repositories, and the sample is small ([METR 2025](sources/metr-early-2025-experienced-developer-rct.md)).
- METR's 2026 follow-up (57 developers, 800+ tasks) reports estimates it calls very weak evidence, with a selection problem: 30-50% of developers withheld tasks they did not want to do without AI. METR says it can no longer measure the effect with this design. The sign convention of the numbers was not checked. So there is no reliable measurement in either direction for 2026; the withheld tasks are behavioural evidence that developers value AI, not a productivity measurement ([METR 2026](sources/metr-2026-uplift-design-update.md)).
- The DORA 2025 report associates AI adoption with higher throughput but lower delivery stability unless strong testing, version control and fast feedback exist. Survey-based, self-reported, only launch pages read ([DORA 2025](sources/dora-2025-ai-assisted-software-development.md)).

**AI weakness at design-level qualities: supported but thinly.**
- GitClear's analysis of 211M changed lines shows cloned lines rising (8.3% to 12.3%, 2021-2024) and refactored code falling from 25% to under 10%. Vendor, observational, cannot isolate AI-authored lines ([GitClear](sources/gitclear-2025-ai-code-quality.md)).
- A 2026 preprint claims AI-generated code is bloated and tightly coupled, that decay grows with volume and with model capability, and that functional correctness and detailed prompts do not prevent it. Only the abstract was read, and sample and models are not recorded ([AI-generated smells](sources/arxiv-ai-generated-smells-code-and-architecture.md)).
- A registered report with a similar-sounding title has no results and must not be cited as evidence ([registered report](sources/arxiv-ai-writes-code-humans-pay-the-debt.md)).

Taken together: the sources agree on a rough behavioural pattern (humans keep design, planning and steering; agents write code) and mostly agree that strategic attention matters more when generation is cheap. They do not establish that AI is reliably good at tactical work. Several point the other way: test tampering, unrequested scope, code-level taste gaps, a measured slowdown, and the need to keep tactical skill in order to supervise (the "paradox of supervision" in the Anthropic study, along with skill-atrophy worry). The junior-pipeline worry in Pocock's thread is an opinion with no evidence attached in the sources.

## Connection to AI coding

- The original Ousterhout distinction reappears in AI form as a risk: an agent optimised to make things work, at speed, resembles a tactical tornado, and the summarised evidence on duplication, coupling and reduced refactoring is consistent with that. This is a reading across sources; no source tests it directly.
- The strategic side that the sources actually name is a mix: architecture and design, planning and specification, harness and process design, coordination of agents, and verification. These are not the same thing as Ousterhout's strategic programming, which concerns the design quality of code one writes. The word covers different concerns across sources.
- Several sources tie architecture to how well AI works: DORA finds gains in loosely coupled systems, Böckeler says committing to a topology makes a harness more achievable, and Pocock argues deep modules help both humans and AI. The first two are summarised; Pocock's is a talk summary.

## Gaps and unverified points

- Nearly everything was read via summaries; no full primary reading of the book, the talk video, the X thread, the DORA report or the two preprints.
- No source tests the split itself, for example by comparing outcomes when humans do strategic work and delegate tactical work versus other arrangements.
- Pocock's own X thread date and the video and transcript date are not established for the transcript.
- The METR numbers' sign convention, the DORA figures and the preprint sample sizes are unchecked.
- Whether the split holds for less experienced developers, non-web or non-Java code, or later models is not covered.
